## **Finding 1 \- The long-term costs are not very intuitive. Users can only see the monthly price.**

**Where:**The pricing title display area and promotional labels on the three contract cards (Screenshot 1\) ("\$15.0/month", "\$21.0/month", "\$31.1/month")

**What the user sees and does:** Users are attracted by the single, small monthly allocation figures. On all the package cards, the mandatory binding total price over 12 months is not calculated or presented on the surface of the core decision-making process. In the lowest price tier, users have to calculate and deduce by themselves: \$7.50 per month for the first 6 months, and the monthly fee doubles to S\$15.0 from the 7th to the 12th month, with a total expenditure of \$135 over 12 months. The psychological reference point of half-price establishment (\$7.50) causes the cost in the 7th month to automatically increase, resulting in an abrupt sense of deprivation and loss.

**Principle:** W4 framing effect (Kahneman, 2011, ch. 34, p. 364); W4 Reference point and loss aversion (Kahneman, 2011, ch. 26, pp. 281–284); W5 Local vs global mental accounting (Shafir & Thaler, 2006, pp. 702–704).

**Mechanism, whose goal wins:** A smaller monthly figure may make the commitment seem easier to afford. Requiring users to calculate the full term increases comparison effort, Acquisition may benefit the provider, while a budget focused user may miss the size of the commitment. This is a plausible mechanism, not evidence that all users are misled.

**Where:** In the "After contract period", box at the bottom of each card (as shown in screenshot 2), it is below the list of green-checked benefits and above the "View Details" option.

They rarely pay attention to the small print \- after the 12-month contract period expires, the data quota will be automatically reduced, while the monthly deduction amount remains unchanged.

**Principle:** W4 Default effect (Kahneman, 2011, ch. 34, pp. 373–374); W4 Status quo bias (Kahneman, 2011, ch. 27, pp. 290–292); W5 Planner-Doer conflict and hot-cold empathy gap (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008, ch. 2, pp. 41–43); W7 Perceptual disfluency via fine print (Petrova, Schwarz, & Song, 2012, pp. 66–67).

**Mechanism, whose goal wins:**This design effectively takes advantage of users' cognitive inertia and limited memory. By placing users in the default rule of "unchanged price and reduced quota". The platform's implicit profit prevailed; subscription users suffered losses in unexpressed service rights.

**Principle:** W3 Prior expectation setting (Ariely, 2008, ch. 9, pp. 158–164); W5 Mindless consumption cues (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008, ch. 2, pp. 43–45).

**Mechanism, whose goal wins:** The label "Unlimited" causes consumers to overestimate their sense of security from avoiding "excessive fines". Although the card lists a 800GB limit, the qualitative commitment ("Unlimited") directly contradicts the quantitative limitation ("Limited to 800GB"). The platform induces users to choose extremely expensive packages

**Finding 4 \- The plan is filled with numerous complicated items, the information architecture is chaotic**

**Where:** The detailed specification area in the middle of the three cards, which contains 8 to 10 rows of text with identical font size and visual weight, listing the benefits (uniform green checkmark icons with compact text).

**What the user sees and does:** Users are faced with detailed information about the extensive package options: local data, multi-region roaming categories, local call minutes, number of text messages. With no hierarchy of information, it's really hard for users to figure out which core benefits justify the price jumps between different tiers. 

**Principle:** W7 Processing disfluency from visual clutter (Petrova, Schwarz, & Song, 2012, pp. 66–68); W5 Mindless choosing under information overload (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008, ch. 2, pp. 43–44)

**Mechanism, whose goal wins:** Information overload creates processing disfluency, inhibiting rational cross-tier comparison. Faced with high cognitive effort, users fall back on heuristic shortcuts, either defaulting to the cheapest tier regardless of fit or picking an overpriced plan out of confusion. The platform benefits from steering users away from optimal, value-conscious choices, while the shopper's goal of easily comparing and selecting the most cost-effective plan is thwarted. The platform's goal wins.

**Finding 5 \- Exit obligations are difficult to assess before signup**

**Where:** At the bottom operation area of each package card: A highly contrasting and extremely prominent main action button (Sign Up Now) is placed, while only a visually weakened and unadorned small text link (View Details) is placed beside it. (Screenshot 5\)

**What the user sees and does:** The interface prompts users to sign up through eye-catching buttons, but it conceals the potential risk of early termination (such as recovering the discounted promotions already enjoyed, paying a fixed penalty, or settling the remaining monthly fees) in a pop-up detail that can only be accessed by clicking "View Details"**.**

**Principle** W5 Account resuscitation (Shafir & Thaler, 2006, pp. 698, 702); W7 Processing fluency(Petrova, Schwarz, & Song, 2012, pp. 66–67).

**Mechanism, whose goal wins:** The card makes signing the lowest-effort action: “Sign Up Now” is a large filled button, while “View Details”, the route to the early-exit terms, is a small text link beside it. Signing needs no extra step (System 1), but learning what leaving early costs needs a deliberate click and reading (System 2 effort), so the exit cost is not visible at the point of decision. The firm’s goal (12-month sign-ups) is served by low friction at entry; the shopper’s goal (knowing the commitment before signing) is served only if they click through. At the point of decision, the firm’s goal wins.

**Finding 6 \- Fragmented and overlapping regional roaming layers**

**Where:** The roaming rights list area in the middle of the three contract cards (Screenshot 6): The roaming quotas are listed independently in four rows: roaming in China and Hong Kong, roaming in countries in Asia, shared large data among Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, and roaming in 210 destinations worldwide. (Screenshot 6\)

**What the user sees and does:** The user is simultaneously dealing with four roaming data pools that overlap with each other (for example: Hong Kong, Malaysia, and Thailand all fall under multiple of these zones). Without referring to the external pop-up details, there is no indication on the interface regarding the priority of charge deductions between different data pools, nor is it listed which specific countries are included in "Asia-Pacific" or "210 regions". The user may overestimate the actual travel guarantee available, but unable to calculate exactly how much data they can use when traveling to a specific country.

**Principle:**  W4 Partitioned framing and mental accounting (Kahneman, 2011, ch. 34, pp. 364, 371–372); W7 Conceptual and imagery disfluency (Petrova, Schwarz, & Song, 2012, pp. 65–68; Lee & Aaker, 2004, pp. 206–207).

**Mechanism, whose goal wins:** The interface lists four independent traffic pools according to different geographical areas, which triggers multiple isolated regional accounts. However, due to overlapping geographical boundaries and the lack of priority deduction explanations resulting in the inefficient processing , users find it difficult to integrate them into a clear "inclusive account" specifically for a single travel destination to rationally assess the actual available amount for this trip.

Where: The $15 card’s promotion labels, including “[Non ST Port-in] 50% off 6 Mths” and “[New] 50% off 6 Mths”. (Screenshot 7)

What the user sees and does: The discount is easy to notice, but the label requires the user to decode difficult-to-understand abbreviations: “ST”, “Port-in”. Someone changing providers may not know whether their current provider qualifies. Reading the discount is therefore easier than checking eligibility.

Principle: Processing fluency (Petrova, Schwarz, & Song, 2012, pp. 66-67)

Mechanism, whose goal wins: Users can understand that they can save half the price. However, when faced with a rare abbreviation like "Non ST Port-in", their minds find it too difficult to decode. According to the processing disfluency principle: if something is difficult to read, one is reluctant to think about it, users will simply skip the qualification review and subconsciously believe that they can also enjoy this discount.

Finding 10: Plan cards list raw prices and specs without indicating relative value or target audience 

Where: The plan titles and feature lists across the three contract cards. (Screenshot 10)

What the user sees and does: Users are presented with three cards that simply list monthly prices and raw technical specs (e.g., “500GB local data”, “500 mins”). The interface lacks any guidance on which plan offers the best value or which plan suits what kind of person. Consequently, users struggle to evaluate the real-life fit of these plans.

Principle: W9 Identity signaling in product domains (Berger & Heath, 2007) and W10 Cultural differences in holistic vs. analytic thinking (Nisbett, 2003).

Mechanism, whose goal wins: Western analytic thinking tends to isolate object attributes like raw GBs, whereas East Asian holistic thinking evaluates information by looking at the broader context and relationships. Additionally, consumers use products to express their identity, which is ineffective when a product is framed purely as a cold utility. By merely listing prices and specs without explaining the value or target audience, the platform strips away the context. This forces users into a purely analytical comparison where they rely on flawed heuristics—either under-buying out of strict price sensitivity or guessing arbitrarily due to choice overload. The platform fails to reliably steer users toward higher-value tiers, and users experience decision friction while missing out on plans that actually match their lifestyle. Neither side wins.
